The big question

One carbon-12 atom has a mass of 1.993 × 10⁻²³ g. How many carbon-12 atoms are in exactly 12 g?

Make a prediction first:

That's the Avogadro constant! 12 g of carbon-12 contains 6.022 × 10²³ atoms — exactly one mole. Because the mole is linked to the atomic mass scale like this, the relative mass of any particle (Ar or Mr) is also the mass in grams of one mole of it.

Teacher notes

Learning intentions. Students can explain that the mole is a counting unit (6.022 × 10²³ particles); calculate M from Ar values; distinguish atoms, molecules and formula units; and convert between mass, amount and number of particles using n = m/M and N = nNA.

Misconceptions targeted.

  • "A mole is a mass" / "1 mole always weighs the same" — Stations 1 and 5 (dozen eggs vs dozen watermelons; the balance).
  • "A mole of water contains 6.022 × 10²³ atoms" — Station 3 (atom inventory) and quiz atom-count questions.
  • Multiplying instead of dividing by M, or skipping the mole step when going mass → particles — the quiz detects these specific errors and responds to them.
  • "NaCl is made of molecules" — Station 3 labels it a formula unit of ions.

Suggested sequence. Work through stations 1–6 in order (about 30–40 min), pausing at each "Think" box for pair discussion. Use station 7 for independent practice or as an exit ticket (e.g. "get a streak of 5").

Note on definitions. Since 2019 the SI defines the mole by fixing NA = 6.022 140 76 × 10²³ mol⁻¹ exactly; 12 g of carbon-12 is now one mole to within about one part in a billion. Station 2 uses the historical carbon-12 link because it explains why Ar values double as molar masses. Simplified Ar values are used throughout.
